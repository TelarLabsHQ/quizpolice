    What is a logical fallacy?

    A logical fallacy is a mistake in reasoning: an argument that sounds convincing but doesn’t actually support its conclusion. Fallacies show up everywhere, from group chats and ads to debates, news comments and even research papers. Learning to spot them helps you write stronger essays, argue more fairly, and avoid being fooled.

    One important point: an argument with a fallacy can still have a true conclusion. The fallacy only means that argument doesn’t prove it. Saying “your argument has a fallacy, so you’re wrong” is a fallacy too.

    12 common fallacies, with examples

    1. Straw man

    Twisting someone’s point into a weaker or more extreme version, then attacking that instead.

    Example: “You want fewer cars downtown? So you want to ban cars completely?”

    How to respond: Restate their real point first: “I think you’re saying X. Is that right?”

    2. False dilemma

    Pretending there are only two options when there are more.

    Example: “Either we cancel the trip or everyone gets soaked.” (You could bring umbrellas or change the plan.)

    How to respond: Ask: “Are these really the only two options?” and name a third.

    3. Slippery slope

    Claiming one small step will lead to a chain of extreme results, without showing why.

    Example: “If we allow one late submission, nobody will ever submit on time again.”

    How to respond: Ask for evidence that each step actually leads to the next.

    4. Ad hominem

    Attacking the person making the argument instead of the argument itself.

    Example: “Don’t listen to his idea about the schedule. He’s always late himself.”

    How to respond: Separate the person from the claim: would the argument be wrong if someone else made it?

    5. Appeal to authority

    Treating someone’s opinion as proof, especially when they aren’t an expert on the topic.

    Example: “A famous singer says this phone is the most secure, so it is.”

    How to respond: Check whether the person is an expert in this topic, and what the evidence says.

    6. Hasty generalization

    Drawing a big conclusion from too few examples.

    Example: “The first two restaurants on this street were bad, so the whole street is bad.”

    How to respond: Ask how many examples there are, and whether they represent the whole group.

    7. Bandwagon

    Saying something is true or good because many people believe or do it.

    Example: “Everyone’s taking this elective, so it must be the most useful one.”

    How to respond: Ask why it’s popular, and whether popularity says anything about whether it’s true.

    8. False cause

    Assuming that because B happened after A, A must have caused B.

    Example: “I started using a new pen and my grades went up, so the pen helped.”

    How to respond: Look for other explanations and for evidence beyond timing.

    9. Circular reasoning

    Using the conclusion as the reason for the conclusion.

    Example: “This source is reliable because it’s a source you can rely on.”

    How to respond: Ask: “What’s the reason, apart from the claim itself?”

    10. Red herring

    Changing the subject to something that doesn’t answer the question.

    Example: Asked about a rent increase, the landlord talks about the new lobby paint.

    How to respond: Bring it back: “That’s interesting, but it doesn’t answer the question.”

    11. Tu quoque

    Rejecting advice or criticism because the other person does the same thing (“you do it too”).

    Example: “You can’t tell me to save money. You bought a new phone last month.”

    How to respond: Ask whether the advice is good, regardless of who gives it.

    12. Appeal to nature

    Assuming something is good because it’s “natural”, or bad because it isn’t.

    Example: “It’s natural, so it’s healthy.”

    How to respond: Judge the thing by its effects, not by whether it’s natural or man-made.

    Why this matters in college

    • Essays and research papers: professors look for arguments supported by evidence. Fallacies are one of the most common reasons a strong-sounding paragraph loses marks.
    • Debates, seminars and group projects: naming a fallacy calmly (“that feels like a false dilemma, are there other options?”) moves a discussion forward without attacking anyone.
    • Everyday life: ads, viral posts and sales pitches often rely on bandwagon, false cause and appeals to authority. Spotting them is a practical defense.

    Tips for avoiding fallacies in your own writing

    • For each claim, ask: what’s my evidence, and is it enough?
    • Describe opposing views fairly before you answer them.
    • Be careful with words like always, never, everyone and only.
    • Look for other explanations before claiming one thing caused another.
    • Read your argument as if you were your toughest critic.

    For teachers

    Spot the Fallacy works as a warm-up for critical thinking, philosophy, English, debate or media literacy classes. Ask students to play a round, then pick the example they found hardest and explain it to a partner, or write their own example of each fallacy. It’s free, needs no accounts and works on phones. Scores stay on the student’s device.

    Questions

    Can one argument have more than one fallacy? Yes. Some overlap, like ad hominem and tu quoque. In the game, the answer choices never include two fallacies that would both be correct.

    Are there more than 12 fallacies? Many more. These 12 are among the most common and the most useful to recognize first.
