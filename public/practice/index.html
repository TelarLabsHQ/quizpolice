Why short practice works

A few minutes of practice on most days beats a long session once a week. These games are built on four ideas that learning researchers have studied for decades, explained here in plain words:

  • Pulling answers out of memory. Each time you bring an answer back from memory, instead of just reading it, it becomes easier to find the next time. This is called retrieval practice, and it’s why every game asks you to answer, not just look.
  • Spacing it out. Coming back to the same facts after a day or two is more effective than repeating them many times in one sitting. That’s why the rounds are short: they’re meant to be played again tomorrow.
  • Mixing it up. Practicing different kinds of questions together makes you think about which method to use, which is exactly what a real test asks of you. Try the Mix it up levels and the All eras and World options.
  • Feedback right away. Every game shows the right answer immediately and lists what you missed at the end, so mistakes get fixed before they stick.

For more on studying this way, read How to Study for a Quiz: 7 Tips That Actually Work.

A simple 5-day practice plan

  1. Day 1: play one round and write down your score and the answers you missed.
  2. Day 2: play the same level again. Before you start, try to recall yesterday’s missed answers without looking.
  3. Day 3: move to a mixed level or a new region or era.
  4. Day 4: go back to your first level. Most people beat their Day 1 score by now.
  5. Day 5: teach someone else one thing you learned this week. Explaining it is the best test of whether you really know it.

For teachers

Share a game link with your class as a warm-up or homework. Students don’t need accounts, and their scores are saved only on their own device. Some ways teachers use the games:

  • Lesson starter: project a game on the board and let the class answer together for the first five minutes.
  • Homework with a target: “Play three rounds this week and write down your best score.” It takes little time and students can see their own progress.
  • Stations: during group work, one station plays a round on a shared tablet while others work on paper.
  • Discussion: after Spot the Fallacy or World History Timeline, ask students to explain one answer to a partner, or to write a new question of their own.
  • Early finishers: students who finish a task early can practice quietly instead of waiting.

Before a real online quiz, share the student checklist so students know how to prepare their device. When it’s time for the quiz itself, QuizPolice lets you monitor Google Forms, Microsoft Forms and Wayground quizzes for free.

For parents

  • Keep it short. Two or three rounds a day is plenty. Stop while it’s still fun.
  • Praise effort and progress, not just high scores: “You beat yesterday’s score” works better than “You’re so smart”.
  • Play together. Take turns on Geography Practice or World History Timeline and talk about the answers. You’ll both learn something.
  • Look at the missed answers. The list at the end of each round tells you exactly what to practice next.
